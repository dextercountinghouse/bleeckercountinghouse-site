# Cold read: Bleecker Counting House
*Notes from a GC / specialty interiors owner (~$40M, ~90 people, Sage 100 Contractor + Procore + spreadsheets). Ten minutes between meetings.*

## 1. First 30 seconds (Home only)

"Data analysis, reporting and operational systems for established firms." A data shop; that headline could be anybody. "Fractional" I take to mean part-time, like a fractional CFO. The subhead is better: "We connect your systems, check them against the books, and build what the business needs on top." "Check them against the books" is what made me stop. My problem isn't a lack of reports; it's not trusting the ones I have.

Who it's for: the Home tab doesn't say construction, but the example question "Why did Atlantic Ave fall to 4.8%?" and the first example card ("Jobs are closing four to six points below the margin we bid. Every project manager has a reason, but we can't see a pattern.") sound like my Monday meeting. So probably for me, though I worked that out from the examples, not from anything the page told me. The next step looks like "Get in touch". I'd click "See examples" first.

Small things: the first card has no title, and a floating "never billed" label means nothing on its own. "Models" in the fact strip made me wonder whether this is an AI pitch.

## 2. Tab by tab

**What we do.** Clear and plain. The 13-week cash forecast and "crew capacity against booked work" I'd actually use. Under "Who we work with", contractors come first: "Job costs, billing against work done, change orders and retainage." That's my business in one line. Then the list keeps going: restoration, distributors, property managers, professional services, bookkeeping firms. So they'll work for anyone, which is less convincing than a construction-only firm. Nowhere do they name Sage or Procore. That's the first thing I looked for.

**How we work.** "Like an in-house analyst: in your systems, with your people, on the weekly schedule of the business." Clear. "Working in the first weeks, not months" is a real promise, and I'll hold them to it. "The same principal on every call" sounds good, but read with the About page it means one person. "A renamed job is handled on our side" matters: my PMs rename jobs constantly.

**Engagement.** Fixed-fee assessment, fixed monthly fee, "No hourly billing." Good, I hate open meters. But there isn't a single number on the page, not even a range. "Systems — Custom technical work for your business, whatever it needs" sounds like the place where scope creep lives. The terms are the most trustworthy part of the site: "don't use shared passwords", "If the check fails, the report waits until it's fixed", "Everything we build is documented and belongs to you."

**Questions.** The accountant answer is sensible. "For a monthly fee instead of a salary" is the right comparison, but without a number it's just a claim. "Where you already pay for software, we'll use it" is good, because I'm not buying another subscription.

**Examples.** This is where they won me over. "Extra work agreed on site, noted in the daily log and never turned into a change order." That's happening in my company right now, I'd bet money on it. Then "labor coded to the wrong phase, which hid overruns until each job closed": also us. The sample weekly report for "Ashcourt Builders" is the best thing on the site. "Approved changes not billed", "Retainage ready to bill", "Three subcontractors have billed $53,235 beyond their subcontracts with no approved change." I'd forward that page to my controller today. I skimmed the other two. Then the letdown: "Sample companies. The figures are made up." I appreciate the honesty, but it means there's no proof on the page.

**About.** Very thin. One name, "Dexter Vandersall", and "Background in research data analysis, report writing and application development." Nothing about construction, no years in the business, no clients, no photo, no LinkedIn, no phone. Where's the "Counting House"? It sounds like a bank or a 19th-century bookkeeper.

**Get in touch.** "Bring us one question your numbers can't answer yet." Good, low-commitment ask. Email only, though: no phone, no calendar link.

## 3. Words and phrases

**Confused or irritated me:** "Fractional data analysis" (I think I get it, but it's jargon). "Operational systems." "Models" and "Models that do the routine work" (is this AI? reading my contracts with what?). "Official access" (does Sage 100 Contractor even have that? Ours lives on a server in the office). "One clean data source." "Integrations, automations." "Principal" (just say "me"). "Reports and dashboard" (singular reads like a typo).

**Felt exactly right:** "check them against the books", "Every figure tied back to the ledger", "Every project manager has a reason, but we can't see a pattern", "no one's job is to line them up", "Earned, not yet billed", "Billed ahead of the work", "Retainage ready to bill", "No hourly billing", "belongs to you".

## 4. Unanswered questions and first-call objections

- What does it cost? Give me the assessment fee and a monthly range.
- Have you actually connected to Sage 100 Contractor and Procore before? How, given our Sage install is on-premise?
- Who are your real clients? Can I talk to one contractor?
- You're one person. What happens if you're sick, on vacation or quit? Who else touches my data?
- What are the "models"? Does my job-cost data or my subs' pay apps go into ChatGPT or similar? Insurance, NDA, any security certification?
- How much of my controller's and PMs' time does this take in the first month?
- Will your numbers match my CPA's WIP schedule, the one my bonding company sees? If they don't, which one wins?
- How long is the assessment, is there a minimum term, and what do I get at the end?
- Do you understand construction, or did you learn it from the examples you wrote?

## 5. Credibility

**Believable:** the examples are specific in a way only someone who has looked at contractor data would write. The weekly report uses our vocabulary correctly. Read-only access, the no-shared-passwords rule, data deleted at the end, and "the report waits until it's fixed" all show they've thought about what makes owners nervous.

**Missing:** any real client, testimonial, reference or logo. Any construction background for the principal. Prices. Named software (Sage, Procore, QuickBooks, anything). A face and a phone number. Right now it's a well-written site from one person I can't verify, so my friend's referral is doing most of the work.

## 6. Do I email?

Yes, mostly because my friend sent it and the change-order example hit close to home. The ask is small and they told me exactly what to send. I'd keep it short and make them answer the hard questions first:

> Subject: Change orders and margin fade, $40M GC
>
> Hi Dexter, [friend] passed along your site. We're a GC and specialty interiors firm, about $40M and 90 people. We run Sage 100 Contractor (on-premise), Procore, and a lot of PM spreadsheets. My question: how much field extra work never becomes a billed change order, and where does our margin go between bid and closeout?
>
> Before a call, could you send (1) your assessment fee and a typical monthly range, (2) whether you've connected to Sage 100 Contractor and Procore before, and (3) one contractor client I could speak with?
>
> Thanks,

## 7. What I'd tell my friend

It's basically one guy who acts as a part-time data analyst for companies like ours. He hooks into your accounting and project software read-only, lines the systems up so jobs and cost codes match, checks everything against the general ledger, and sends you a weekly report and a review call. The report covers unbilled work, change orders nobody invoiced, retainage you can bill, subs billing past their contracts, and margin fade by job. He'll also build small tools and answer one-off questions in writing. Fixed fee to start, then monthly, no hourly. The sample report is excellent. But there are no prices, no real clients and no construction résumé on the site, so I want to know how you found him and whether he's done this for you.

# Cold read: Bleecker Counting House, from a controller's chair

*Controller / acting CFO, mechanical and specialty contractor, about $65M revenue. CPA, CFMA member. Owner asked: "Worth a call?"*

## 1. First 30 seconds (Home tab only)

The headline, "Data analysis, reporting and operational systems for established firms," could belong to anyone. The line under it is what stopped me: "We connect your systems, check them against the books, and build what the business needs on top." The diagram makes the idea concrete. On the left is "Your systems": accounting, project management ("Jobs, budgets, change orders"), timesheets, "Pay applications, estimates." In the middle is "One source — Reconciled to the ledger." On the right is "What you get": a weekly review, written answers, a dashboard and alerts. The fact strip adds "Read-only, through each system's official access" and "Every figure tied back to the ledger." Whoever wrote this has listened to controllers.

So: outsourced analysis and reporting on top of the systems we already run, for mid-sized firms without a data team. The first example is a contractor whose "Jobs are closing four to six points below the margin we bid." That's us. On fit, probably yes. The one word that caught me was "Models" ("Matching records, reading documents, forecasting"). Does that mean AI?

## 2. Tab by tab

**Home.** Covered above. The example's flow, "Bid → Field → Change order → Invoice → Ledger," is the real change-order path. "Someone reading the numbers" is the right promise, but it doesn't say who.

**What we do.** Concrete: "Cash over the next 13 weeks," "estimate accuracy by type of work," "reminders for invoices, retainage and change orders." The contractor line, "Job costs, billing against work done, change orders and retainage," uses the right vocabulary. Vague: "keep one clean source." Mild red flag: six industries listed. I'd rather see depth than breadth.

**How we work.** "The same principal on every call" is honest. It is also the key-person risk, stated in one line. "Working in the first weeks, not months" stays a sales claim until I see it on our ERP. "A new system or a renamed job is handled on our side" is good. That's exactly how my last consultant's spreadsheets broke.

**Engagement.** The strongest tab. A fixed-fee assessment, then a fixed monthly fee, "No hourly billing." The Terms headings are crisp: "Checked before it goes out," "Your data stays where agreed," "You own it," plus "don't use shared passwords" and "If the check fails, the report waits until it's fixed." But there's no dollar figure anywhere, not even a range. "One point of contact" presents key-person risk as a feature. And "Systems… whatever it needs" is open-ended scope.

**Questions.** "Why not use dashboard software?" goes straight at my first failed project: "it won't check it against the books, catch it when a system changes, or tell you why a margin dropped." "What if we stop?" gets a clean answer. On the accountant, "The two roles don't overlap much" is fair, but nothing says how their numbers relate to my month-end WIP.

**Examples.** Honestly labeled: "the companies and figures are made up." The change-order example is the most credible thing on the site.

**About.** The weakest tab, and the one that matters most to me. One principal, with a "Background in research data analysis, report writing and application development." Nothing about accounting, construction, a CPA, years in practice, clients or references. The site says "we" throughout, but only one person is named.

**Get in touch.** "Bring us one question your numbers can't answer yet" is a good, easy ask. Email only, though. No phone, and no address beyond "Bleecker Street."

## 3. Accounting and construction substance

Better than I expected. The sample report puts real WIP concepts in plain English. "Earned, not yet billed" means underbillings. "Billed ahead of the work" means overbillings. I checked the math: earned less billed ($7,551,936 − $7,030,150 = $521,786) equals underbillings less overbillings ($686,953 − $165,167), and the change column foots. The callouts are what I'd want PMs to see on Monday: a change order "approved 41 days ago" still unbilled, subs that "billed $53,235 beyond their subcontracts with no approved change," and retainage that "can now be billed for release." "91% complete and 79% billed" is exactly how I'd put it.

What's loose:
- The header says "Issued 2 November · reconciled to the ledger." October isn't closed on November 2. Reconciled to which ledger, in what state?
- Where do the "Forecast costs" come from? Cost-to-complete is the PMs' call. The site never says who owns it, or whether percent complete is cost-to-cost.
- A "weekly report" compares against "4 Oct," four weeks back.
- "Retainage ready to bill" sits under "active jobs," but the note says "three finished jobs." And is "Approved changes not billed" included in underbillings, or on top of it?
- In the margin example, most of the rest of the gap is put down to "labor coded to the wrong phase." Miscoding doesn't lose money. It hides overruns, which they half-admit.

What impressed me: extra work "noted in the daily log and never turned into a change order," caught by a nightly match against the CO log and escalated after "three days." That's a real control.

## 4. Risk questions

| Risk | Answered? |
|---|---|
| Who does the work | Partly. "Same principal," plus unnamed "Models." Does a person review model output before it reaches me? |
| Key-person | Not addressed. One named principal, no backup, no continuity plan. |
| Security | Thin. "Secure environment set up for you," but nothing on hosting, encryption, SOC 2, insurance or NDA. If the "Models" are third-party AI, do our job and payroll data go to those vendors? |
| Access | Good: read-only, official access, no shared passwords. But an "approvals workflow" or invoice reminders sounds like more than read-only. |
| Verification | Promised, but no method given. |
| Pricing | The structure is clear. The amounts are missing. |
| Exit | Answered on paper: documented, ours, data deleted. But if the "one source" lives in their environment, does deleting it leave us with nothing? |

## 5. Language

**Precise and trustworthy:** "check them against the books," "If the check fails, the report waits until it's fixed," "don't use shared passwords," "13-week cash forecast," "pay applications," "approved 41 days ago," "No hourly billing," "Your data stays where agreed."

**Jargon, sales or vague:** "Fractional data analysis," "operational systems," "established firms," "Models," "one clean source," "secure environment," "whatever it needs," "Working in the first weeks, not months," "handled on our side." The name "Counting House" suggests bookkeeping, but the site shows no accounting credentials to back that up.

## 6. What I'd tell the owner

Worth a 30-minute call, not a commitment. They clearly understand where contractor margin leaks, and the site speaks to both of our failed projects. What's unproven is who is behind it and how our data is handled. My first questions:

1. **Who touches our data, and what are "Models"?** Where is it hosted, does any of it go to AI vendors, what's your security and insurance, and what happens if you're unavailable for a month?
2. **How exactly do you tie to the ledger?** Walk me through reconciling a weekly report to a GL I close monthly and to my WIP schedule. Where does cost-to-complete come from? How do we avoid a second set of numbers that conflicts with what the bank and surety see? Which ERPs have you done this on?
3. **What does it cost, and what do we own at exit?** Give me ranges for the assessment and the monthly fee, and show me documentation from a past build.

## 7. What this firm does, in my words

Bleecker Counting House looks like a one-principal firm in New York. It connects read-only to a company's accounting, project, timesheet and document systems, matches the records into one source reconciled to the general ledger, and then delivers a verified weekly report and call, a dashboard with alerts, written answers to management questions, and short-term cash and margin forecasts. On a scoped basis it also builds custom internal tools. Pricing is a fixed-fee assessment followed by a monthly retainer. For contractors, the pitch is catching money lost between systems: unbilled change orders, underbillings, sub overbillings and retainage left sitting. It says the work is documented and stays with the client. It shows no accounting credentials, no client history and no security specifics.

## Problems that sit between systems
Worked examples on sample companies, to show the kind of problem we take on and how it gets found. They're not client case studies: the companies and figures are made up.

### Where the margin went
[Renovation contractor · 14 finished jobs · sample company]
"Jobs are closing four to six points below the margin we bid. Every project manager has a reason, but we can't see a pattern."
[Why no one could see it] Estimates, daily logs, change orders, hours and billing each sit in a different system. Each one looks right on its own, and no one's job is to line them up.
[What we did]
- Connected the four systems read-only and matched every job, phase and cost code across them. Several jobs went by three different names.
- Rebuilt each finished job from bid to final invoice: what was estimated, what was done, what was billed.
- Followed the gap. Most of it wasn't materials or labor rates. It was extra work agreed on site, noted in the daily log and never turned into a change order.
[What it found] $212,400 of extra work across 9 of the 14 jobs had no change order, and $148,900 of it was never billed. That accounts for about 3.8 points of the missing margin. Most of the rest was labor coded to the wrong phase, which hid overruns until each job closed.
[Figures] $148,900 extra work never billed · 3.8 pts of the missing margin · 9 of 14 jobs affected
[What runs now] Each night, daily log entries that describe extra work are matched against the change order log. Anything still without a change order after three days goes to the project manager and comes up in the weekly review.

### Freight that never got charged back
[Building-products distributor · 1,100 accounts · sample company]
"Gross margin looks fine by product line, but net margin keeps sliding and we can't tell which customers are costing us."
[Why no one could see it] Quotes assume standard freight. Liftgate, redelivery and reweigh charges arrive weeks later on the carrier's invoice and go to one general freight account.
[What we did]
- Matched each line of every carrier invoice to its shipment and order.
- Compared freight charged to the customer with freight paid, by customer and by lane.
[What it found] Small accounts with residential delivery were losing about 6% of revenue to freight. Forty customers made up most of it.
[What runs now] Carrier invoices are matched every night. Orders where freight paid runs past freight charged are flagged, and the quote sheet uses actual freight by lane.

### Repairs that should have been billed to tenants
[Property manager · 6 commercial buildings · sample company]
"Year-end reconciliations keep coming in short. Owners want to know why recoverable expenses are down when maintenance is up."
[Why no one could see it] What a tenant pays for is set in each lease, which lives as a PDF. The bookkeeper codes invoices without them to hand.
[What we did]
- Read the recovery clauses from every lease into one table of what's recoverable, by building and by tenant.
- Matched work orders to vendor invoices and to how each was coded.
[What it found] About 11% of recoverable repair spend had been coded as non-recoverable across all six buildings.
[What runs now] New invoices are checked against the lease table as they're coded. Mismatches go to the bookkeeper before the month closes.
