What we do

Areas of work

I

Questions answered

Owners and managers send questions as they come up, and we answer them in writing using the company's own data. Common ones: why a job lost money, which clients pay late, what a certain type of work actually costs. Most come out of the weekly review.

Typical outputs

A written answer with the numbers behind it, and a recommendation where the data supports one.

II

Forecasting and planning

Projections based on the work you have now. Cash over the next 13 weeks, margin on jobs in progress, crew capacity against booked work, and pricing guidance from past jobs.

Typical outputs

A 13-week cash forecast, forecast margin by job, capacity against booked work, estimate accuracy by type of work.

III

Reports and dashboard

A weekly report pulled from your systems and checked against the books before it goes out. The same numbers are on a dashboard you can open any time, with alerts if something moves past a limit you set.

Typical outputs

Weekly report, monthly profitability report, client dashboard, alerts.

IV

Systems and tools

Most companies have the same job or client entered in several places. We match those records, flag the differences and keep one clean source. On top of it we build the tools the business needs, such as a project archive, an estimating tool, an approvals workflow, or reminders for invoices, retainage and change orders, and keep them running.

Typical outputs

One clean data source, reconciliation across systems, in-house databases and tools, scheduled admin tasks.

Who we work withEstablished companies running on several systems, without a data team of their ownUsually that means accounting software, one or two industry systems, and spreadsheets kept by different people.

  • Property managersOwner statements, vendor invoices and cash by property.

  • Bookkeeping firmsReporting for their own clients, built on the books they already keep.

Questions

Common questions

How is this different from our accountant?+

Your accountant keeps the books accurate and handles filings. We use those books along with your operational data, like estimates, jobs and timesheets, to report on how the business is running. Most clients keep their accountant. The two roles don't overlap much.

What do we need to do?+

Give read-only access to your accounting and project systems once, and put any exports or documents in a shared folder. After that it's mostly the weekly call. If a system changes or a file is missing, we take care of it.

Where is our data kept?+

We only read your data and never change it. It's stored in a secure environment set up just for you, or inside your own systems, and deleted when we stop working together.

How is it priced?+

The assessment is a fixed fee based on scope. After that, Reporting and Fractional analyst work are a fixed monthly fee, and Systems work is priced once it's scoped in the assessment. No hourly billing.

Why not hire an analyst?+

A new analyst would first need the data connected and cleaned up, and usually a data engineer to keep it that way. We come with both and the systems already built, for a monthly fee instead of a salary.

Why not use dashboard software?+

Software can display your data, but it won't check it against the books, catch it when a system changes, or tell you why a margin dropped. Where you already pay for software, we'll use it.

How do we get started?+

Email us a short description of the business, the systems you use, and a question you'd like answered. We'll set up a call and scope the assessment from there.

What if we stop?+

Everything we've built is documented and stays with you. We hand over or close the connections and delete any data we hold.

Examples

Problems that sit between systems

Worked examples on sample companies, to show the kind of problem we take on and how it gets found. They're not client case studies: the companies and figures are made up.

Renovation contractor · 14 finished jobs · sample company

Where the margin went

"Jobs are closing four to six points below the margin we bid. Every project manager has a reason, but we can't see a pattern."

Why no one could see it

Estimates, daily logs, change orders, hours and billing each sit in a different system. Each one looks right on its own, and no one's job is to line them up.

What we did
  • Connected the four systems read-only and matched every job, phase and cost code across them. Several jobs went by three different names.
  • Rebuilt each finished job from bid to final invoice: what was estimated, what was done, what was billed.
  • Followed the gap. Most of it wasn't materials or labor rates. It was extra work agreed on site, noted in the daily log and never turned into a change order.
What it found

$212,400 of extra work across 9 of the 14 jobs had no change order, and $148,900 of it was never billed. That accounts for about 3.8 points of the missing margin. Most of the rest was labor coded to the wrong phase, which hid overruns until each job closed.

What runs nowEach night, daily log entries that describe extra work are matched against the change order log. Anything still without a change order after three days goes to the project manager and comes up in the weekly review.
Building-products distributor · 1,100 accounts · sample company

Freight that never got charged back

"Gross margin looks fine by product line, but net margin keeps sliding and we can't tell which customers are costing us."

Why no one could see it

Quotes assume standard freight. Liftgate, redelivery and reweigh charges arrive weeks later on the carrier's invoice and go to one general freight account.

What we did
  • Matched each line of every carrier invoice to its shipment and order.
  • Compared freight charged to the customer with freight paid, by customer and by lane.
What it found

Small accounts with residential delivery were losing about 6% of revenue to freight. Forty customers made up most of it.

What runs nowCarrier invoices are matched every night. Orders where freight paid runs past freight charged are flagged, and the quote sheet uses actual freight by lane.
Property manager · 6 commercial buildings · sample company

Repairs that should have been billed to tenants

"Year-end reconciliations keep coming in short. Owners want to know why recoverable expenses are down when maintenance is up."

Why no one could see it

What a tenant pays for is set in each lease, which lives as a PDF. The bookkeeper codes invoices without them to hand.

What we did
  • Read the recovery clauses from every lease into one table of what's recoverable, by building and by tenant.
  • Matched work orders to vendor invoices and to how each was coded.
What it found

About 11% of recoverable repair spend had been coded as non-recoverable across all six buildings.

What runs nowNew invoices are checked against the lease table as they're coded. Mismatches go to the bookkeeper before the month closes.
